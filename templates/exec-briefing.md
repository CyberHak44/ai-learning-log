# Ten-minute executive briefing: the mobile boardroom

Draft v0.1 for discussion, 2026-11-04. A short, practical briefing for executives. Focus on behaviour and reputation, not technology.

## Minutes 0 to 2: why it matters

- "We treat cars like private meeting rooms. Most of them are not."
- Dashcams, phones, smartwatches and the car's own connected services can capture what is said and send it to third-party servers.
- One overheard sentence, about a deal, or "Bob is a jerk, we are letting him go", leaves the company's control the moment it is spoken.

## Minutes 2 to 5: the rules

| Stop | Do instead |
| --- | --- |
| Sensitive calls in taxis or ride-hailing | Use the approved transport vendor for sensitive travel |
| Real names on calls in transit | Use project code names |
| Voice dictation in vehicles | Type in the approved encrypted messaging app |

## Minutes 5 to 8: two scenarios

- **Approved vehicle, partition down.** Raise it. Treat the driver as a third party. The NDA is the legal backstop, not the control.
- **Stuck at the airport, taking a taxi.** Say nothing sensitive. If it cannot wait, type it.

## Minutes 8 to 10: questions and the takeaway

- "If the wheels are moving and you are not in a checked vehicle, assume you are being recorded."
- Leave a small card with the three rules and the security contact number.

## Delivery notes

- No technical jargon. Talk about reputation, leaks and liability.
- Acknowledge the friction. Present the rules as protecting the executive's own reputation.

## Thrashing it out

| # | Draft said | Challenge | Where we landed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Used emoji timers and coloured symbols | Distracting, and off-brand for client material | Plain headings |
| 2 | "Delete ride-hailing apps from company devices" | Overreach that will be ignored | Dropped; the rule is about conversations, not apps |
| 3 | Example remark: "Bob is a jerk" | Too informal for executives? | Kept. Executives recognise it instantly, and know what is really said is worse. It makes the point that privacy protects the person talked about, not only the speaker |
