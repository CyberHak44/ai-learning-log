STEP 1 OF 9 · THE AUDIT LOOP

An audit is a loop, not a report

The small-firm security audit has eight moves: scope every company and trading name, tailor the 42 controls to the client, gather evidence before asking questions, score each control, grade its severity, write one fix per gap, feed the fixes into the plan, and keep it from slipping. Then it starts again next month. A report that is read once and filed changes nothing; the loop is what the client pays for.

Think of it as

An MOT for a business. The tester does not take your word that the brakes work; he checks, writes down what fails, and you come back next year. We do it monthly, because a business changes faster than a car.

First scores are lowUnder the evidence rule most small firms start well under 50%. That is the honest starting line, and the monthly re-score shows the climb.Why audit ourselves firstWe sell this. A client will ask what we found in our own firm. "We run it on our own firm every month" is the answer.

STEP 2 OF 9 · EVIDENCE BEATS PROMISES

Green means "I can show you", not "I think so"

Each control gets one of four scores. G: evidenced and current. A: partly evidenced, or true only on the owner's word. R: no evidence, failed or overdue. NA: does not apply to this client. The hard rule: an owner's statement on its own never scores green. So a laptop that is encrypted but has no screenshot on file scores amber.

Think of it as

A gas safety certificate. The landlord saying "the boiler is fine" is not enough for the tenant or the insurer; the dated certificate is.

Say it in one line

"If you can't show it, we score it red. That's not harsh, it's what your auditor or your bank will do."

STEP 3 OF 9 · NEVER MISS A DATE

The Renewal Register: every date, three warnings

In founder-led firms the failures that hurt first are usually missed dates: a lapsed trade licence, late accounts leading to a strike-off notice, an insurance policy that quietly expired. None is hard. Each was simply not on a list with a warning. The Renewal Register holds every legal and commercial date with a lead time (how long the renewal takes) and works out the start-by date. Its status turns amber at 60 days, red at 30, and overdue after the date.

Think of it as

The DVLA reminder for car tax, but for everything: licence, visa, accounts, tax, insurance, domains, certificates.

  1. Open the audit workbook, Renewal Register sheet, and list every date: licence, visas, accounts, confirmation statement, tax returns, insurance, domains, certificates.
  2. Any line without a date shows DATE MISSING. Find the date; that search is often where the gap is found.
  3. Approve the dates, then each one gets calendar reminders at 60, 30 and 7 days.
  4. Rule: any line that is RED and not started takes a fixed daily block until it is done.
Say it in one line

"Most small firms don't fail on security, they fail on dates. Every date goes on one register with three warnings."

STEP 4 OF 9 · MULTI-FACTOR AUTHENTICATION

MFA: a stolen password alone is no longer enough

MFA asks for two different things: something you know (the password) and something you have (your phone's authenticator app, or a hardware key). Passwords leak all the time; the second factor stops the thief at the door. Use an authenticator app or a key, not SMS, where the service allows it, because SMS can be redirected by SIM-swap fraud.

Think of it as

A bank card and PIN. Someone who learns your PIN still needs the card in their hand.

  1. Google Workspace (your mailbox): admin.google.com, Security, Authentication, 2-step verification. Tick "Allow users to turn on 2-step verification", set Enforcement to On, save. Then on your own account: myaccount.google.com, Security, 2-Step Verification, add Google Authenticator or a passkey.
  2. Every other account: list them first (free zone or company registry portals, banks, accounting, code hosting, social profiles, AI tools, domain registrar, DNS host, tax portals). Open each one's security settings and turn on the authenticator option.
  3. Save the recovery codes each service gives you into the password manager (step 5), never in email or on the desktop.
  4. Evidence: one line per account in the account list: service, MFA method, date switched on.
Say it in one line

"Every account that touches company or client data has MFA with an authenticator app, and I can show you the list."

STEP 5 OF 9 · PASSWORD MANAGER

One strong master password; a different password everywhere else

A password manager makes and remembers a long, unique password for every site, fills it in for you, and keeps MFA recovery codes safe. You remember one long passphrase. A common audit finding is a portal password reset by a supplier and sent in an ordinary email: that is the problem a manager removes, because nothing needs to be typed, remembered or emailed.

Think of it as

A key safe by the door. Every key is different, and you only carry the one code that opens the safe.

  1. Pick one business-grade manager (for example Bitwarden or 1Password). Business plans cost a few dollars a user a month [guessing on current prices; check before buying].
  2. Choose the master passphrase: four or five unrelated words, at least 20 characters. Write it once on paper, kept at home in a safe place, never photographed.
  3. Turn on MFA for the password manager itself.
  4. Install the browser extension and the phone app. Move every account in, changing each password to a generated one as you go. Start with email, banking, the registry portal and the domain registrar.
  5. Store each service's MFA recovery codes and the BitLocker recovery key (step 6) as secure notes.
  6. Evidence: a screenshot of the vault's item count and the security report showing no reused passwords.
Say it in one line

"No one in our firm knows or reuses a password; they are generated and held in a managed vault with MFA."

STEP 6 OF 9 · BITLOCKER AND DEVICE PROTECTION

BitLocker: a lost laptop becomes a lost piece of hardware, not a data breach

BitLocker encrypts the whole Windows drive. The key is held in a chip on the motherboard (the TPM) and released only when Windows starts normally. If the laptop is stolen and the drive is pulled out, the data on it is unreadable. The one thing you must keep safe is the recovery key, a 48-digit number Windows asks for if the hardware changes. Lose it and you lose your own data.

Think of it as

A safe bolted inside the car. A thief can take the car, but not open the safe; and the spare safe key lives at home, not in the glovebox.

  1. Check your Windows edition: Settings, System, About. Windows 11 Pro has full BitLocker. Windows 11 Home has "Device encryption" on supported hardware, which uses the same technology.
  2. Pro: Start, type "Manage BitLocker", open it, choose "Turn on BitLocker" for drive C:. Home: Settings, Privacy and security, Device encryption, switch On.
  3. Back up the recovery key: choose "Save to your Microsoft account" or "Save to a file" (save the file to a USB stick, copy the number into the password manager, then delete the file from the stick). Never keep it only on the laptop itself.
  4. Choose "Encrypt entire drive" and "New encryption mode" when asked. Let it finish; you can keep working.
  5. Prove it: right-click Start, Terminal (Admin), run manage-bde -status C:. You want "Protection On" and "Percentage Encrypted: 100%". Screenshot it for the evidence file.
  6. Same session, two more checks: Windows Security, Virus and threat protection: "No actions needed". Settings, Windows Update: automatic updates on. Phone: Settings shows a passcode set (modern iPhones and Android phones encrypt automatically once a passcode is on). Screenshot each.
Say it in one line

"Every device is encrypted with BitLocker, patched and running Defender; the recovery keys are held separately in our vault, and I have the status screenshots."

STEP 7 OF 9 · SPF, DKIM AND DMARC

Stop anyone sending email that looks like it came from you

Three DNS records protect a domain. SPF lists the servers allowed to send your mail. DKIM adds a digital signature to every message. DMARC tells receiving mail servers what to do when a message fails both: nothing (p=none, just report), put it in spam (quarantine), or refuse it (reject). A domain with SPF but no DKIM and no DMARC lets anyone send a convincing invoice "from" you to a client. It is one of the commonest gaps in small firms.

Think of it as

SPF is the list of approved couriers, DKIM is the wax seal on the letter, and DMARC is your instruction to the post office: "if the seal is broken, bin it".

  1. DKIM in Google Workspace: admin.google.com, Apps, Google Workspace, Gmail, Authenticate email. Select the domain, Generate new record (2048-bit, selector "google"). Copy the TXT value.
  2. Add it in DNS at your DNS host: open the domain's DNS settings and Add new record: type TXT, name google._domainkey, value as copied. Wait up to an hour, then press "Start authentication" in Google.
  3. DMARC: add TXT, name _dmarc, value v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc@yourdomain.com. Reports arrive daily; once only Google appears as a sender, move to quarantine, then reject.
  4. Domains that do not send mail (old brands, parked domains): TXT at the root v=spf1 -all and _dmarc = v=DMARC1; p=reject;. That tells the world no genuine mail ever comes from it.
  5. Check: use any public DMARC checker, or the dns.google lookup, and screenshot the result for every domain you own, sending or not.
Say it in one line

"Our domains publish SPF, DKIM and an enforced DMARC policy, so nobody can impersonate us to our clients, and we check yours in the first hour of an audit."

STEP 8 OF 9 · BACKUPS THAT RESTORE

A backup only counts once you have restored from it

The rule of thumb is 3-2-1: three copies of your data, on two different kinds of storage, with one kept away from the working device. OneDrive is sync, not a full backup: if ransomware encrypts a file, sync can copy the damage. Your second copy fixes that only if it is encrypted, not permanently connected, and you have proved you can get a file back.

Think of it as

A fire drill. Nobody knows if the exits work until you walk out through them.

  1. Once a quarter, pick one real file from the second copy (for example the latest register workbook).
  2. Restore it to a test folder, open it, and check that it is the right version and opens cleanly.
  3. Record date, file, source, time taken and result in the backup log. Delete the test copy.
  4. If it fails, that is an incident (log it) and the fix goes on the Renewal Register.
Say it in one line

"We keep an independent, encrypted copy and we prove a restore every quarter; the log is your evidence."

STEP 9 OF 9 · MAKE IT A HABIT

Six small habits stop it slipping

Controls decay. What keeps them alive is a rhythm that does not depend on remembering: (1) the Renewal Register; (2) calendar reminders at 60, 30 and 7 days; (3) a daily start-of-day check naming anything amber or red; (4) one line in the weekly scorecard, reds open and reds closed; (5) the escalation rule, where a red date takes a fixed daily block; (6) a monthly re-score on the first Friday, with statutory filings handed to one accountant so they never depend on you alone.

Think of it as

Brushing your teeth. Nobody needs willpower for it, because it is tied to a time of day.

For clientsThe same six habits are part of the audit product. The client gets the register, the reminders and a monthly re-score from us.For your own firmSet the first re-score date before you close the audit. A sensible first target: no Critical reds within 30 days.
