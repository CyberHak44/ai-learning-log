STEP 1 OF 7 · WHAT A FORTIGATE DOES

One box at the front door of the network

A FortiGate sits between the business and the internet. Everything going in or out passes through it. It does four jobs in one box: it routes traffic, it filters traffic with rules, it inspects what is inside the traffic for threats, and it builds VPN tunnels so remote people and other offices can connect safely. Fortinet's operating system on the box is called FortiOS.

Think of it as

The reception desk of an office building. It decides who may come in (rules), checks bags at the door (inspection), gives staff a keycard for the side door (VPN), and writes everyone in the visitor book (logs).

The ports on the box are interfaces. Here, wan1 faces the internet and internal faces the office.

Say it in one line

"A FortiGate is a next-generation firewall: it routes, filters with policies, inspects content for threats, and terminates VPNs, all in one box."

STEP 2 OF 7 · FIREWALL POLICIES

The rule list is read top to bottom. First match wins.

Every connection is checked against the firewall policy list, starting at the top. The first policy that matches decides what happens, and the FortiGate stops reading. If nothing matches, the hidden last rule, the implicit deny, blocks it. So order matters: a broad "allow" rule above a specific "block" rule means the block never runs.

Think of it as

A bouncer with a list. He reads from the top and acts on the first line that fits you. If your name is on no line, you do not get in.

Say it in one line

"FortiGate policies are processed top-down, first match wins, and anything unmatched hits the implicit deny, so specific rules go above general ones."

STEP 3 OF 7 · NAT

Many private addresses share one public address

Office devices use private addresses (like 192.168.1.10) that the internet cannot route to. Source NAT swaps the private address for the FortiGate's public address on the way out, and swaps it back on the reply. To let the internet reach one machine inside, such as a camera recorder, you use a Virtual IP (VIP), which is destination NAT: public address and port in, private address out.

Think of it as

A company switchboard. Staff call out from one main number (source NAT). Outside callers ring the main number and ask for extension 20 (VIP).

Addresses shown are documentation examples, not real ones. A VIP on its own does nothing: it also needs a firewall policy that allows the traffic.

Say it in one line

"Source NAT hides inside addresses behind the firewall's public IP going out; a Virtual IP plus a policy publishes one inside service coming in."

STEP 4 OF 7 · SECURITY PROFILES

A policy says "allowed". Profiles check what is inside.

Accepting traffic is not enough: the allowed web traffic could still carry malware or go to a phishing site. Security profiles attached to the policy inspect the content. Most web traffic is encrypted (HTTPS), so the FortiGate can only see inside if SSL/TLS deep inspection is on, and that needs the FortiGate's certificate trusted on every staff device.

ChoicePlain meaningTrade-off
Flow-basedChecks traffic as it streams pastFaster, the usual default
Proxy-basedHolds the whole file, checks it, then sends it onMore thorough, uses more of the box
Certificate inspectionReads only the site name from the certificateNo certificate needed on devices, sees little
Deep inspectionDecrypts, inspects, re-encryptsSees everything; FortiGate CA must be trusted on devices; exempt banking and health sites
Say it in one line

"The policy decides if traffic is allowed; security profiles decide if what is inside is safe, and without deep inspection most of it is encrypted and unseen."

STEP 5 OF 7 · VPN

A private tunnel across the public internet

A VPN encrypts traffic between two points so the internet in between cannot read or change it. Site-to-site IPsec joins two offices permanently. Remote access lets one person on a laptop or phone join the office network. IPsec is set up in two phases: phase 1 (IKE) lets the two ends prove who they are and agree keys; phase 2 builds the tunnels that carry the actual traffic.

Think of it as

An armoured van between two branches. Phase 1 is the guards checking each other's ID and agreeing the code word. Phase 2 is loading the van and driving.

Fortinet has been moving remote access away from SSL VPN towards IPsec in recent FortiOS releases. Check which FortiOS version your course and exam cover.

Say it in one line

"IPsec builds an encrypted tunnel in two phases, identity and keys first, then the data tunnels; site-to-site joins offices, remote access joins people, always with MFA."

STEP 6 OF 7 · LOGS AND MONITORING

If it isn't logged, it didn't happen

Every decision the FortiGate makes can be logged. Three kinds matter: traffic logs (who talked to whom, allowed or denied), security logs (what a profile caught: virus, blocked site, IPS hit) and event logs (changes to the box itself: logins, config changes, VPN up or down). Logs on the box itself are limited, so they are usually sent to FortiAnalyzer, FortiCloud or a SIEM where they are kept and searched.

Say it in one line

"Traffic logs show who talked to whom, security logs show what we caught, event logs show who changed the firewall; ship them off the box so they survive and can be searched."

STEP 7 OF 7 · PROTECT THE FIREWALL ITSELF

The firewall is the most attacked box you own

Attackers target firewalls directly because one weak firewall opens the whole network. Fortinet flaws regularly appear on CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list. So the admin side of the FortiGate needs its own protection, and this is also where your GRC work meets the firewall: the settings below are the evidence an ISO 27001 auditor asks for.

Say it in one line

"Lock down the firewall first: no admin from the internet, MFA, current firmware, backed-up config and logs off the box; each one maps to an ISO 27001 control."
