STEP 1 OF 7 · THE BIG PICTURE

What is IEC 62443, in one picture?

IT security protects information. Control system security protects things that move, heat, cool, open and stop. A hacked laptop leaks data. A hacked BMS can turn off cooling to a data room or unlock a fire exit. IEC 62443 is the rulebook for securing those control systems. The standard calls them IACS: industrial automation and control systems. In a building, that means BMS, lighting control, CCTV, access control, AV and lift gateways.

Think of it as

Fire safety for networks. A building is split into fire compartments so a fire in one room cannot spread to the whole floor. IEC 62443 splits a control network into zones so an attack in one system cannot spread to the rest.

Same discipline, different priority. In OT, availability and safety come first.

Say it in one line

"ISO 27001 protects information. IEC 62443 protects the systems that keep a building running and its people safe."

STEP 2 OF 7 · WHO EACH PART IS FOR

Three roles, four groups of parts

The standard is a family of documents, called parts. Do not try to learn them all. Learn who each part is written for. There are three roles, and each role has its own parts. Part numbers read as group-number, so 2-4 is group 2, document 4.

Think of it as

Building a hotel. The owner decides how safe it must be. The contractor installs and maintains it. The manufacturer makes the controllers and cameras. Each has different homework.

3-3 is used by everyone: suppliers build to it, integrators configure to it, owners specify it. It sits with the supplier here because it describes what a system can do.

STEP 3 OF 7 · ZONES AND CONDUITS

Split the building into zones. Control every path between them.

A zone is a group of systems that need the same level of protection. A conduit is a controlled path between two zones, usually a firewall rule, a VPN or a gateway. The rule is simple: zones are separated, and nothing crosses between them except through a conduit you have documented and control.

Think of it as

Fire compartments and fire doors. The zone is the compartment. The conduit is the fire door: it exists on purpose, it is the only way through, and someone checks it closes properly.

Say it in one line

"Every system goes in a zone, every path between zones is a conduit with an owner and a rule, and anything else, like a 4G router in the plant room, is a hole."

STEP 4 OF 7 · SECURITY LEVELS

How strong does each zone need to be? Size it to the attacker.

A security level, SL 1 to SL 4, says what kind of attacker a zone must be able to resist. You do not give every zone SL 4, just as you do not put a bank vault door on a broom cupboard. You pick the level that matches who might attack and what they could break.

Building examples are from your crosswalk row OT-05: a hotel BMS zone at SL-T 2, a hospital smoke-control zone at SL-T 3.

TermPlain meaningWho sets it
SL-TTarget. The level a zone needs, decided in the risk assessmentAsset owner (3-2)
SL-CCapability. The level a product or system can reach when set up properlySupplier (3-3, 4-2)
SL-AAchieved. The level actually in place on site todayMeasured in audit
The test

If SL-A is lower than SL-T, you have a gap to close. If the product's SL-C is lower than SL-T, no amount of configuration will get you there: you need a compensating control or a different product.

Say it in one line

"The owner sets a target level per zone from the risk, the supplier says what their kit can reach, and the audit checks what is really achieved."

STEP 5 OF 7 · THE SEVEN FOUNDATIONAL REQUIREMENTS

Seven questions every zone must answer

Every detailed requirement in 3-3 (called an SR, system requirement) sits under one of seven foundational requirements, FR 1 to FR 7. Learn the seven as plain questions. Each card shows a building example and the matching row in your crosswalk.

Memory hook, in order: Who are you, what may you do, is it intact, is it private, where can it flow, do we notice, does it keep running.

Say it in one line

"Identify, authorise, protect integrity, protect confidentiality, restrict flow, respond in time, stay available. In OT, the last one usually matters most."

STEP 6 OF 7 · THE RISK ASSESSMENT WORKFLOW (3-2)

How an owner gets from "a building" to "a target level per zone"

Part 3-2 gives seven steps, called ZCR 1 to ZCR 7 (zone and conduit requirements). This is the workflow you would run as a consultant. It is also where steps 3 and 4 come together.

Why it matters to you

Your crosswalk rows OT-03, OT-04 and OT-05 are ZCR 2, 3 and 5. ISO 27001 scores risk on information. 3-2 scores it on safety, environment and physical impact too, per zone. That difference is why OT-03 is only Partial.

Say it in one line

"Scope it, rough risk, draw zones, check against tolerable risk, detailed risk with a target level per zone, write it down, owner signs."

Your own finding: 15 of 31 covered
